# How we calculated things

## Observer and terrain

- **Observer height · 1.8 m above local ground.** We chose this as a simple standing-person viewpoint for the landscape and the Sun and Earth visibility estimates. Adjust it in Moon Explorer’s Settings to model a different viewing or antenna height.
- **Shadow height · 2 m above local ground.** We chose this as a representative height for equipment when calculating site-wide sunlight and casting terrain shadows. Settings also lets you change this value.
- **Moon radius · 1,737.4 km.** We use NASA’s mean radius as a spherical reference, then add the mapped elevation and the chosen observer or shadow height. For example, a site at −100 m elevation with a 1.8 m observer has an eye elevation of −98.2 m relative to that sphere. [NASA Moon Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html)
- **Elevation maps.** We use SLDEM2015, which combines NASA’s LOLA laser altimetry and SELENE/Kaguya stereo imagery, within 60° of the equator. We use LOLA polar grids at higher latitudes and coarser LOLA grids for distant terrain. Resolution and coverage vary by location. [NASA SLDEM2015](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/54) · [NASA LOLA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/lro/lola/)
- **Terrain horizon.** At each bearing, we take the highest elevation angle of the sampled terrain, including lunar curvature. The local skyline checks terrain up to 300 km away, typically at 0.25° bearing intervals. The site-wide horizon maps use 1° intervals to 300 km. Orbital and landscape shadows use separate nested terrain grids reaching up to 600 km. The finest grid spacing is 1.5 m, and detail still depends on the source elevation map. The landscape keeps faint relief in shadow to make its shape readable.

## Sun, Earth, and mission time

- **Positions and pointing.** JPL SPICE and the DE440 ephemeris give the apparent position of the Sun and Earth from the observer, with light-time and stellar aberration corrections. Azimuth runs clockwise from local north and elevation is measured up from level. The calendar dates are UTC. Seasonal effects and lunar libration enter through the computed body positions and lunar orientation at each time. [JPL planetary and lunar ephemerides](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/eph_export.html) · [NAIF SPICE observer calculation](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/FORTRAN/spicelib/spkcpo.html)
- **Rise, set, and Earth communications.** Sunrise and sunset mark when the first or last part of the Sun’s disc clears the terrain. Direct-to-Earth windows use the centre of Earth. For example, Earth at 2.4° elevation above a 1.8° skyline has 0.6° of terrain clearance. The communication window estimates geometric line of sight. Ground-station schedules, antenna performance, and link budgets are outside the model.
- **Timing and totals.** We scan for horizon crossings every 2 minutes for missions of up to 31 days and every 5 minutes for longer missions, then refine crossings within those brackets. Short events between samples may be missed. Mission visibility totals add the windows within the selected dates, so 6 hours in a 24-hour mission is 25%. When ephemeris coverage allows, the next rise or set can be searched for up to 30 days past mission end. Playback and the comparison report use the selected playback interval, which defaults to 15 minutes. The comparison report counts visible portions of Earth’s disc, while the communications section uses its centre.

## Solar intensity and energy

- **Solar intensity** is the direct sunlight striking a terrain slope at the selected time, in W/m². We start with 1,361 W/m² at one astronomical unit, scale by the inverse square of the distance to the Sun, and account for the visible solar disc and angle of incidence. The selected-time rendering also accounts for apparent eclipses by Earth. [IAU Resolution B3](https://www.iau.org/common/Uploaded%20files/IAUGA2015-Resolution-B3-recommended-nominal-conversion.pdf)
- **Solar energy received** adds up direct sunlight over the mission using the simulated sample times and the exact mission end. Its unit is kWh/m². For example, 300 W/m² sustained for two hours is 0.6 kWh/m². The mission energy estimate uses terrain shading and slope and excludes eclipses, reflected sunlight, and equipment efficiency.
- **Time in darkness** shows how long each point has been continuously dark through the selected playback time, beginning at mission start. The map uses sampled, quantized fractions of the visible solar disc. In the backend’s site-wide sunlight statistics, darkness means less than 0.1% of the disc is visible.
